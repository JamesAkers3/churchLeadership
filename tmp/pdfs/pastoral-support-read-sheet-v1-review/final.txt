A PASTOR CAN BE KNOWN AND STILL UNSUPPORTED.




People can know your name, hear your
sermons, and depend on your leadership
without knowing what you carry.
Visibility can hide isolation. Recognition and support are not the same thing. A Pastor may be surrounded by
people, appreciated by the church, and still have no safe place to speak honestly or share the weight.
The three figures in the visual come from separate survey questions. They are not a scale and they do not
describe one group moving through three stages. They do tell a Pastor who feels alone, you are not the
only one. They also ask the church whether the people around the role know the person carrying it.
Who knows the soul beneath your role?

Jesus invited people closer
In Gethsemane, Jesus went apart to pray to the Father. He brought Peter, James, and John farther with Him
and named the sorrow He was carrying. The wider circle of disciples remained nearby, but these three were
invited closer and asked to keep watch.
They fell asleep. Their failure does not make His request a weakness. Jesus showed that strength and
honest need can exist together.
Gethsemane shows the difference between having people
nearby and allowing trusted people close enough to see the cost.         “Therefore, a Christian

The weight can be shared without diminishing
the calling.
In Exodus 17, Moses did not win by hiding his exhaustion. Aaron and Hur gave him a place to sit and held up
his hands. Joshua led the people in the battle. No one replaced Moses, and Moses did not carry every part
alone.
The calling may be personal, but the weight was never meant to be carried alone.
The same pattern appears around Paul. The Philippian church shared in his troubles, sent help more than
once, and sent Epaphroditus to care for his needs. Aristarchus, a believer from Thessalonica, stayed close
enough to accompany Paul and later be identified as his fellow prisoner. Compassion became provision,
presence, partnership, and shared responsibility.

Compassion takes different forms
No act of compassion is too small to matter, and no burden is too great for the body of
Christ to face together. We do not have to do everything. We do have to faithfully play our
part.
Some will hold up the Pastor’s arms. Some will take on responsibilities the Pastor has been carrying alone.
Some will bring resources. Some will create room for rest. Some will cover the pulpit, make the phone calls,
provide the meals, or simply remain close when the road becomes difficult.

Care becomes real when someone carries part of
the weight.
The Pastor is called to shepherd the congregation, but the congregation is also called to care for those who
shepherd them. As people respond to the calling Christ has placed on their lives, they begin to notice,
encourage, strengthen, and help carry one another, including their Pastor.
Not every weight belongs on the Pastor’s shoulders simply because they have been
willing to carry it.

Questions worth sitting with
1.   Who is close enough to see both the fruit of the Pastor’s leadership and what it costs to carry it?
2.   What responsibilities has the Pastor continued carrying because no one else has stepped forward or been
     prepared to carry them?
3.   What practical act of care would create meaningful support now, before a crisis makes the need impossible
     to ignore?

Putting this into practice
Begin with one safe, honest conversation. Do not require the Pastor to disclose personal struggles in a group or
make them responsible for designing their own care.
Ask what weight can be shared now. The response may be a protected day of rest, peer support, counseling,
covering the pulpit, changing a schedule, ending needless work, or placing a real responsibility into capable
hands. Agree on who will act, what support they need, and when you will check back.
Caring for a Pastor is not merely a kind gesture. It is one way the body of Christ faithfully
cares for its own.

Moving into the next chapter
Support asks who is close enough to know the person carrying the role. The next conversation asks how a Pastor
remains in Christ, allows trusted people closer, and brings others alongside the work.
That brings us to Who Are You Bringing With You?

Presenter notes
•   Let people examine the visual before explaining the statistics. Ask what they notice and where they feel tension.
•   Say plainly that the Barna figures are separate survey measures, not a diagnosis, progression, or mathematical contrast.
    Their purpose here is recognition and conversation, not shock.
•   Keep the conversation from becoming another assignment for the Pastor. The board, staff, volunteers, and congregation
    should ask what part Christ is calling them to carry.
•   Do not pressure a Pastor to disclose pain publicly. Offer private paths for conversation, explain confidentiality limits, and
    encourage qualified professional or denominational support when appropriate.
•   Honor the Pastor’s faithfulness without making overwork untouchable. A willingness to carry weight does not prove that
    the weight still belongs on the Pastor’s shoulders.
•   Invite concrete care. “We appreciate you” is meaningful, but appreciation is not the same as rest, friendship, shared
    responsibility, spiritual support, or a safe place to tell the truth.
•   Keep the two human quotations visually and theologically subordinate to Scripture. Bonhoeffer supports the mutual need
    within Christian community. Stanley names a leadership consequence of keeping struggle hidden.

Scripture and content boundaries
•   Mark 14:32-42: Jesus brought Peter, James, and John farther into Gethsemane and asked them to keep watch. Judas
    had already separated from the group. Do not present the scene as all twelve disciples occupying identical circles.
•   Exodus 17:8-13: Aaron and Hur held up Moses’ hands, while Joshua led the people in battle. The passage shows distinct
    people carrying different parts; it should not be used to place Moses or a modern Pastor beyond accountability.
•   Philippians 2:25-30; 4:14-18: The Philippian church supported Paul and sent Epaphroditus. While Paul was in
    Thessalonica, the Philippians sent aid more than once.
•   Acts 20:4; 27:2; Colossians 4:10: Aristarchus was from Thessalonica, accompanied Paul, and was later identified as his
    fellow prisoner. Do not state that the entire Thessalonian church served Paul in prison.
•   1 Thessalonians 5:11-14; Galatians 6:2: These passages ground mutual encouragement, help for the weak, patience,
    and shared burdens. Read the passages from a Bible rather than presenting our application as a quotation from Scripture.
•   The 1-3-12 language is a relational principle, not a ranking system or a required headcount. The fuller explanation
    belongs in the next chapter.
