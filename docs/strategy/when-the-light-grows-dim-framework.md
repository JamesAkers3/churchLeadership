# When the Light Grows Dim

Pastoral care, honest assessment, and real change for leaders who have carried too much for too long.

> **Misalignment scatters passion. Burnout drains the capacity to carry it.**

The passion–vision–mission work asks whether a church's energy has focus and direction. This is a different conversation. This asks what happens when the pastor has been depleted, wounded, isolated, or repeatedly exposed to crisis without enough time, support, or safety to recover.

## The fire they keep running into

> **Some pastors are not losing their passion. They are losing the capacity to carry it.**
>
> They have run into the fire again and again—absorbing crisis, grief, conflict, betrayal, and the pain of people they love. Then they returned with smoke still in their lungs and were sent straight back into the next fire.
>
> The calling may still be alive. The vision may still be clear. The mission may still matter. But the person carrying it is exhausted, wounded, and running on empty.
>
> You do not restore that pastor by handing them another assignment, a sharper vision statement, or a bigger expectation. You help them stop, breathe, tell the truth, share the weight, receive care, and heal.

The light may not be gone. The person carrying it may have been burning as the fuel.

Clear vision cannot make up for depleted capacity. Rest without real change is only a pause before the next collapse. We cannot send a wounded pastor back into an unchanged fire.

## The Heartbeat

Jesus never treated people like endless resources.

When the apostles returned from ministry, Jesus let them tell Him what they had done and taught. Then He saw the pressure around them and gave them a direct invitation: come away with Me and rest (Mark 6:30–32).

Jesus regularly withdrew to quiet places to pray, even while the crowds kept growing (Luke 5:15–16). In Gethsemane, He allowed Peter, James, and John to see His sorrow and asked them to stay near (Matthew 26:36–38). He formed the Twelve before He sent them, and He taught them that lasting fruit comes from abiding in Him (Mark 3:13–15; John 15:1–5).

We should not use those passages to reduce Jesus to a slogan about self-care. Scripture does not call Him burned out. His life does show us something important: faithful ministry includes return, honest report, prayer, rest, trusted relationships, shared work, and dependence on God.

The pastor is an under-shepherd. The pastor is not the Savior.

## Know what we are looking at

This work can help a church notice warning signs and find the right care. It cannot diagnose a mental-health condition.

- **Burnout:** The World Health Organization describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It can include exhaustion, growing distance or cynicism toward the work, and reduced effectiveness. WHO does not classify burnout as a medical condition.
- **Compassion fatigue and secondary traumatic stress:** Helpers can be affected by repeated exposure to the trauma and pain of the people they serve. Avoidance, persistent tension, or feeling continually on alert may be warning signs.
- **Moral injury:** A person may carry deep psychological, relational, behavioral, or spiritual distress after doing, witnessing, or failing to prevent something that violated deeply held moral beliefs. Guilt, shame, anger, betrayal, loss of trust, and spiritual disorientation can follow. Moral injury can overlap with PTSD, but it is not itself a psychiatric diagnosis.
- **PTSD and other conditions:** Diagnosis belongs to a qualified mental-health professional after a proper clinical assessment.

Unless a clinician has made a diagnosis, the better phrase for this work is **trauma-related stress symptoms**.

Our role is to notice what is happening, protect confidentiality, connect the pastor with qualified help, and help the church change harmful conditions. We do not diagnose or provide trauma treatment.

## Seven movements toward care and repair

These are not seven boxes to check. Healing does not move in a straight line, and the church's desire to get back to normal must never set the pastor's pace.

### 1. See the dimming without shaming the pastor

Something has changed. Do not rush to call it laziness, weakness, disobedience, or a lost calling.

Watch for patterns such as:

- Exhaustion that does not lift
- Dread, numbness, cynicism, irritability, or detachment
- A fading sense of meaning or effectiveness
- Trouble sleeping, concentrating, deciding, or recovering after ordinary demands
- Avoidance, intrusive memories, hypervigilance, or feeling continually on alert
- Guilt, shame, anger, betrayal, or spiritual disorientation after painful ministry events
- Isolation, secrecy, compulsive overwork, or an inability to stop responding
- A repeated return from crisis with no time to process what happened

Do not ask only, “What is wrong with this pastor?” Ask, “What has happened to this pastor? What have they been carrying? What has this church learned to call normal?”

### 2. Stop the next unnecessary trip into the fire

The immediate goal is not better performance. It is safety.

That may mean removing nonessential meetings, decisions, preaching expectations, counseling demands, and after-hours availability. Name someone else to carry the ministry so the pastor is not secretly working while supposedly resting. Protect the pastor from pressure to explain everything publicly. Decide who truly needs to know. Bring in urgent medical, mental-health, or crisis support when needed.

We cannot praise a pastor for running into every fire and then punish that pastor when they can no longer breathe.

### 3. Give the pastor somewhere safe to return

The apostles returned to Jesus and told Him what happened. A pastor needs that kind of safe return.

The first questions are simple:

- What happened?
- What keeps happening?
- What has it cost you spiritually, emotionally, physically, relationally, and vocationally?
- Where are you carrying grief, guilt, anger, shame, fear, or betrayal?
- What feels impossible to resolve?
- What have you been afraid to say?
- Why has it felt unsafe to say it?

An advisor can help make room for this conversation. Trauma processing and treatment belong with qualified professionals.

### 4. Come away and receive whole-person care

Jesus' invitation was concrete. Leave the demand. Come away. Rest.

A vacation may not be enough. Care may include:

- Protected rest with no hidden on-call expectation
- Medical evaluation for physical symptoms, sleep disruption, or other health concerns
- A licensed counselor or trauma-informed mental-health professional
- A trusted spiritual director, pastoral counselor, or mature ministry mentor whose role is care, not evaluation
- Support for the spouse and family without making them responsible for fixing the pastor
- Prayer, worship, Scripture, silence, and time with Christ that are not treated as productivity assignments
- Time to grieve what ministry culture may have told them to minimize

Prayer and professional care are not enemies. Receiving skilled care is not spiritual failure.

### 5. Share the weight

The pastor cannot return to being the place where every need, decision, conflict, and emergency must land.

This is where the 1–3–12 work connects:

- **1:** The pastor honestly stewards health, limits, calling, and capacity under Christ.
- **3:** Trusted people know the truth, notice the warning signs, pray, challenge, encourage, and help lift the pastor's arms.
- **12:** A wider circle, including the three, is formed and trusted with real ministry.

> **The same Holy Spirit that is gifting you, empowering you, teaching you is in them and calling them.**

Sharing ministry is not just a way to become more efficient. Jesus formed and sent people. He did not build a ministry that depended on one visible leader doing everything.

### 6. Change what keeps causing harm

We cannot call a pastor healed because they have learned to tolerate an unhealthy church.

Look honestly at:

- The responsibilities of the pastor, staff, board, and volunteers
- Workload, schedule, days off, vacation, sabbatical, and after-hours boundaries
- Whether responsibility and authority actually match
- Staffing, volunteer development, delegation, and backup coverage
- Which crises truly require the senior pastor
- Board behavior, conflict, trust, and the freedom to disagree
- Unresolved betrayal, abuse, coercion, or institutional harm
- Measures of success that reward visible growth while hiding personal damage
- Systems that drain life or make one person indispensable

The hard question is:

> **Are we helping this pastor heal, or are we helping this pastor tolerate what injured them?**

### 7. Discern the next faithful step

The goal is not to put the pastor back in the same role, at the same pace, as quickly as possible.

The next step may be a gradual return with clear limits and shared responsibility. It may be a redesigned role, a longer leave, a formal sabbatical, continued treatment, a transition to another ministry, or a season outside vocational ministry.

Health, family, calling, trust, clinical guidance, and the changes made by the church all matter. The environment must be safe enough for healing to continue.

Jesus was intentional and specific. “Things will be different” is not enough. Name what has changed. Name who owns each responsibility. Name the limits that will be protected. Name how concerns will be raised before another crisis.

## Four places to look

An outside advisor can help the church examine four connected areas without trying to act as a clinician.

### The pastor

- What can the pastor carry right now?
- What warning signs are present?
- What care is already in place?
- What needs to stop immediately?

### The people around the pastor

- Who knows this pastor well enough to notice change and speak honestly?
- Are there trusted relationships that do not depend on job performance?
- Is the board creating safety, pressure, conflict, or uncertainty?
- How is this affecting the spouse and family?

### The role

- What truly requires the pastor's gifting, authority, or presence?
- What is still being held because of fear, habit, control, or undeveloped leaders?
- How often is the pastor interrupted by preventable emergencies?
- Is the written role different from what the church actually expects?

### The church

- Which systems create life, clarity, shared ownership, and healthy limits?
- Which systems drain people or make one leader indispensable?
- What happens when a leader says no, asks for help, or admits weakness?
- What does the church praise that may actually be a warning sign?
- What must change before a responsible return?

## Lines we will not cross

- We will not diagnose.
- We will not use prayer or Scripture to shame someone away from medical or mental-health care.
- We will not force a pastor to disclose trauma to a board, congregation, advisor, or unsafe person.
- We will explain the limits of confidentiality before sensitive conversations begin.
- We will not treat a fast return or increased productivity as proof of healing.
- We will not prescribe rest while leaving the harmful conditions untouched.
- We will not send a pastor back into an unchanged environment.
- We will not act as the pastor's therapist, physician, attorney, or crisis counselor.
- We will build and use a trusted referral network.

If someone may be in immediate danger or at risk of suicide or self-harm, this is no longer an ordinary advisory conversation. In the United States, call or text **988** or use the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline chat. Call emergency services when there is immediate danger.

## What this work could become

- A pastoral sustainability and ministry-load assessment
- A confidential map of the relationships supporting the pastor
- A board conversation about burnout, compassion fatigue, moral injury, boundaries, and the church's responsibility
- A review of roles, workload, decisions, and interruptions
- A recovery-support plan shaped with the pastor, board, and qualified care providers when appropriate
- A clear path for returning to ministry or redesigning a role
- A local and national network of licensed clinicians, pastoral counselors, spiritual directors, retreat ministries, denominational resources, and peer-support communities

These are ideas to develop later. They are not finished service offers.

## A separate picture

The approved passion–vision–mission visual should remain its own work.

**When the Light Grows Dim** needs a separate image. The lens and mirror may still be aligned while the light reaching them has become weak or unstable. Focus and direction can still be present while the person has been depleted.

The picture of recovery should not be someone simply turning the light back up. It should show the conditions around the person changing. Demand stops. The person is protected. Weight moves to other shoulders. Support comes close. The light returns gradually, not on command.

The center of that visual is:

> **Some pastors are not losing their passion. They are losing the capacity to carry it.**

Its guardrail is:

> **Do not send a wounded pastor back into an unchanged fire.**

## What good looks like

The pastor tells the truth sooner. Trusted people listen without shame or panic. The right care is brought close. The church changes the conditions that are consuming people. Ministry weight is shared. The next step is shaped by faithfulness, health, and calling—not by the institution's need to get back to normal.
