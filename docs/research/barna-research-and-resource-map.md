## What this research confirms

### 1. The pastor may still believe deeply in the calling while struggling under the role

Barna's 2026 research found that confidence in calling had recovered to 58 percent, while the share of pastors who were very satisfied with their vocation had fallen from 72 percent in 2015 to 52 percent in 2026. Barna points to role structure, delegation, and alignment with a pastor's strengths and gifts as part of the gap. [Read the research.](https://www.barna.com/research/pastors-calling-satisfaction-research-2026/)

That gives outside support to language we have already protected:

> Some pastors are not losing their passion. They are losing the capacity to carry it.

It also strengthens the 1–3–12 work. Shared ministry is not only a leadership technique. It can be part of restoring a pastor's ability to live and lead within the calling God has given them.

### 2. Burnout cannot be solved by handing a tired pastor another resource

In Barna's 2026 study of 507 Protestant senior pastors, the highest-rated needs were mental and physical health, close supportive relationships, and financial stability. The most helpful but most difficult actions were extended rest, delegation, and changing the role to fit the pastor's strengths and limits. Resources and curriculum ranked lowest in perceived helpfulness. [Read the research.](https://www.barna.com/research/what-relieves-pastoral-burnout/)

This sharpens the separate **When the Light Grows Dim** framework. We are not trying to give a depleted pastor more homework. We are helping the pastor, board, and staff make room for rest, truth, shared responsibility, boundaries, wise care, and real structural change.

Older Barna findings also show how isolation, weak support, and strained pastor-board relationships are connected with burnout risk and lower ministry satisfaction. [Review the support-system research](https://www.barna.com/research/pastor-support-systems/) and [the pastor-board relationship research](https://www.barna.com/research/healthy-pastors-relationships/).

### 3. Church health needs a wider lens than attendance and giving

Barna's Thriving Church framework names 15 dimensions:

- Connected Community
- Prayer Culture
- Spiritual Formation
- Worship Experience
- Bible Centeredness
- Trusted Leaders
- Faith Sharing
- Holistic Stewardship
- Social Impact
- Serving Others
- Leadership Development
- Future Focused
- Resource Stability
- Data Informed
- Team Health

[See Barna's overview of the 15 dimensions.](https://www.barna.com/thrivingchurchinstitute/)

Several dimensions line up directly with this endeavor: spiritual formation, trusted leaders, faith sharing, social impact, leadership development, future focus, data-informed decisions, and team health.

That does not mean we copy Barna's framework or make it our theology of the church. It means their research can be one useful outside lens when we ask what is healthy, what is being neglected, and where the church's stated priorities do not match its lived reality.

The [ChurchPulse pathway](https://access.barna.com/thriving-church) is also close to the evaluation process we have been describing: gather data, name strengths and opportunities, choose one or two areas of focus, act, and measure again. If we use a self-selected congregational survey, we will clearly state its limits; respondents may overrepresent engaged, willing, and digitally comfortable people. [Review the ChurchPulse sampling caution.](https://assets.gloo.us/assessments/results-tab/understanding-your-data/church-pulse-convenience-sampling-methodology.pdf)

### 4. Community trust is built through proximity, integrity, and ordinary faithfulness

Barna's 2026 trust study found that small churches scored 61 on its trust index, compared with 36 for megachurches. Its practical counsel is to stay local, be known for integrity more than visibility, and treat smallness as a relational advantage. [Read the trust study.](https://www.barna.com/research/trust-in-christian-churches/)

This gives needed depth to the marketing and first-impression work. The goal is not to make a church louder or polish an image that is not true. The public message, visual identity, website, social presence, welcome, and community relationships should tell the same honest story.

Branding cannot manufacture trust. It can either make the truth easier to see or make the confusion worse.

Barna also found that pastors' confidence in their churches' outreach effectiveness fell from 63 percent in 2015 to 39 percent in 2022. Pastors who had considered quitting were more likely to believe their church was ineffective in outreach. [Read the outreach and burnout findings.](https://www.barna.com/research/church-pastor-role/)

For local engagement, loneliness may be a particularly important doorway. Churched and unchurched metro adults both saw churches as well positioned to address it. [Read the local-church study.](https://www.barna.com/research/local-church-role/)

### 5. Technology should serve mission instead of becoming another mission

In Barna's 2026 church-technology study, 95 percent of church leaders said technology opens new ministry opportunities and 94 percent said it helps churches fulfill their mission in today's digital culture. Yet only one in four leaders fell into Barna's most mission-aligned technology group. [Read the study.](https://www.barna.com/research/church-technology-mission/)

The strongest engagement was associated with churches that connected technology to discipleship, community, and ministry goals. That reinforces our passion–vision–mission picture:

> Passion is the energy. Vision brings it into focus. Mission points it somewhere that matters. Impact happens where it lands.

The question is not, “What else can we buy or add?” It is, “How can what we already have help us follow Jesus and serve people more faithfully?”

The public-facing digital review still matters. Barna has long noted that a church website may be an outsider's first or only impression of that church. [Read the internet-presence research.](https://www.barna.com/research/cyber-church-pastors-and-the-internet/)

### 6. Churches need AI guardrails now

Barna's 2026 pastor study reports that 87 percent of pastors use AI in some form. The most common uses include brainstorming, visual creation, biblical or theological research, small-group questions, and administration. At the same time, 71 percent describe themselves as cautious. [Read the pastors-and-AI study.](https://www.barna.com/research/pastors-using-ai-ministry/)

A separate study found that only 5 percent of churches had formal AI guidelines. Leaders' leading concerns included data privacy, plagiarism or message integrity, and the loss of authenticity in preaching or teaching. [Read the church AI-guidelines study.](https://www.barna.com/research/church-leaders-ai-usage-concerns/)

Barna also found that 94 percent of pastors were concerned about AI misinterpreting Scripture. When a passage is genuinely disputed, 60 percent wanted AI to present the major interpretations and their differences without deciding which one is correct. [Read the Bible-interpretation study.](https://www.barna.com/research/ai-scripture-interpretation/)

This supports a clear line for our future framework:

- AI may assist with research, comparison, organization, administration, and early drafts.
- AI must identify uncertainty, show sources, and surface meaningful disagreement.
- AI does not replace prayer, discernment, pastoral presence, spiritual authority, confession, care, or the responsibility to handle Scripture faithfully.
- A pastor remains accountable for every message delivered in their name.
- Private pastoral information, counseling details, prayer requests, personnel matters, and sensitive church data require strict protection.

The tool can serve the calling. It cannot carry the calling.

### 7. Spiritual openness is not the same as discipleship

Barna reports renewed spiritual curiosity alongside weaker long-term measures of formation. Its 2026 ministry trends describe growing openness while warning that discipleship is not keeping pace. [Read the 2026 trends.](https://www.barna.com/research/state-of-the-church-2026-trends/)

That matters for community engagement. Attention, attendance, a successful event, or a positive online response can be a beginning. None of them should be mistaken for formation in Christ.

## What we should build from this

Barna's research suggests five early tools for this endeavor:

1. **A pastor role-and-capacity conversation** — calling, gifts, energy, responsibilities, limits, rest, support, and what can be shared.
2. **A church health listening process** — leaders, congregation, neighbors, observable systems, and a small set of measures that matter.
3. **A community trust and first-impression review** — what the church says, what people experience, how the church is known, and whether local relationships support the public message.
4. **A mission-aligned technology review** — keep, change, stop, or add based on discipleship, connection, community, and stewardship.
5. **A Christ-centered AI covenant** — useful applications, sacred boundaries, privacy, authorship, source checking, human accountability, and regular review.

## The Heartbeat

Research can tell us what people report, where strain is showing, and what patterns deserve attention. It cannot tell the Church whom to follow.

Jesus did not look at the crowd as a market. He saw people. He had compassion. He asked questions. He spoke truth. He formed disciples. He sent them. He received them back. He gave Himself for the lost.

We will use Barna to listen more carefully. We will not use it to replace prayer, Scripture, the leading of the Holy Spirit, or the voice of the people standing in front of us.

The numbers may help us see the field. Christ still sends the laborers.
